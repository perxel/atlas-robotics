The single biggest reason people try stargazing once and give up isn't equipment — it's timing. Step outside, look up for ninety seconds, see "not much," and go back in. Your eyes were never given a chance to actually adjust.

## The twenty-minute rule

Human night vision takes about twenty minutes to fully adapt to darkness, and a single glance at a phone screen resets that clock back to zero. If you're serious about a session, put the phone away — or at minimum switch it to red-light mode — and give your eyes the full twenty minutes before deciding the sky looks empty.

## Start with what's already up there

You don't need to know constellations to have a good first session. The moon rewards even a cheap pair of binoculars with visible craters. Planets read as small, steady, non-twinkling points of light — that steadiness is the tell that separates a planet from a star at a glance.

## Satellites aren't cheating

A surprising number of "stars" that move are satellites, and watching one cross the sky in a slow, even line is genuinely satisfying once you know what you're looking at. It's a good beginner win: something moving, visible to the naked eye, no equipment required.

## Where to actually go

You don't need a dark-sky reserve for a first session — you need distance from the nearest streetlight and about twenty minutes of patience. Save the road trip for once you're hooked.
