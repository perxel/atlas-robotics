Every hiker who's done a winter summit knows the specific kind of gamble it is: the valley forecast and the summit forecast are two different forecasts, and you don't find out which one you're getting until you're already above the tree line.

## Reading a mountain forecast for what it doesn't say

A "cloudy" forecast at the trailhead can mean fog at 2,000 meters and clear sky at 3,000 — the cloud layer has an altitude, and a summit hike is really a bet on where that altitude sits relative to the peak. We'd checked three separate models the night before; two disagreed on exactly this.

## The break

About forty minutes below the summit, the trail crossed into direct sun without warning — the cloud ceiling was right there, a flat gray floor stretching to the horizon with the peak sitting entirely above it. It's a strange kind of quiet up there: no valley sounds carry through cloud the way they carry through clear air.

## What we'd do differently

Start earlier. The break in the clouds held for about ninety minutes before drifting back up to swallow the summit again — we made it down with maybe twenty minutes to spare before we were back in the fog we'd started in.
