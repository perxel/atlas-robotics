A table that photographs well and a table that actually gets used by people mid-conversation are not automatically the same table. The gap between them usually comes down to three decisions made hours before anyone sits down.

## Decide the anchor first

Every table needs one piece that does the visual work for everything else — for us, that's almost always the charcuterie board, built wide and low so it reads as abundant without towering over the other dishes. Once the anchor is placed, everything else gets arranged around it, not the other way around.

## Cook backward from the table, not forward from the kitchen

The biggest table-setting mistake is timing dishes for when they finish cooking instead of when they'll be eaten. We plate the dessert and the cold sides first, hours ahead, and work backward so the one thing still on the stove — usually something steamed, finishing at the last minute — is the only hot addition to an otherwise ready table.

## Leave room for the table to get messy

A table set too precisely stops looking inviting after the first serving spoon moves. We leave visible gaps on purpose — a board half-empty, a stack of plates slightly askew — so the table still looks intentional after twenty minutes of actual use, not just in the first photo.
