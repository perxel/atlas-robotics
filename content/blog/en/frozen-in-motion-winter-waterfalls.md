A waterfall freezing isn't really freezing — it's slowing down, one layer at a time. The outer sheet of water hits cold air first and hardens into ice, while the falls keeps running underneath, often invisibly, for weeks after the first freeze.

## Why some falls freeze and others don't

Volume is the deciding factor more often than temperature. A high-volume falls moves too much water too fast to lose heat evenly, so it often keeps flowing well into a cold snap that would freeze a smaller stream solid within a day. The falls in Chiavenna, Italy freezes in stages — ice building outward from the rock face while the core keeps moving, which is why a "frozen" waterfall in a photo is often still audible if you're standing next to it.

## What it takes to see one

Most frozen falls worth photographing aren't roadside. Reaching them usually means a cold-weather hike over packed snow, sometimes with crampons, and timing the trip for a cold snap that's held for at least a few days — a single freezing night rarely does more than glaze the surface.

## Worth the cold hands

The payoff is a version of a waterfall that only exists for a few weeks a year, and looks nothing like the same falls in July — which is really the whole appeal of chasing winter waterfalls in the first place.
